About TSA Tracker

TSA Tracker is a live airport utility: it shows checkpoint wait times, airport-specific guidance, and recent history so travelers can make a real timing decision instead of guessing.

Last reviewed: {{ copy_updated_label }}

What makes this site useful

The value is in the combination of fresh wait readings, airport-specific context, and the trend line underneath them. The homepage answers the immediate question, the airport pages add checkpoint detail, and the guides explain how to use the data without overthinking it.

Why We Built This

Getting to the airport is stressful enough. Even after you arrive, the useful question is still simple: how long is the security line right now? A useful answer needs source status, freshness, and checkpoint context instead of an unlabeled airport-wide number.

TSA Tracker exists to surface supported airport-published readings when fresh, label fallback estimates when they are not, and present both in a format that is easy to act on before you leave home.

Who Builds TSA Tracker

TSA Tracker is an independent publisher-run site built and maintained by Ben Birkhahn. Ben works across software, automation, data collection, and practical web tools, and built TSA Tracker to turn public airport wait-time data into a focused same-day travel utility.

The project is intentionally narrow: find official airport wait-time sources, normalize them carefully, label what is live versus estimated, and give travelers enough airport-specific context to make a better departure decision.

This is not a government site and it is not affiliated with the TSA or any airport authority. The site reads airport-controlled wait-time systems, labels the source state clearly, and adds editorial guidance around those readings so travelers can make a same-day timing decision.

How the Data Works

For supported integrations, TSA Tracker checks airport-controlled sources approximately every 2 minutes, parses the available feed, and stores timestamped checkpoint and lane readings.

A page labeled live shows the exact capture time for its fresh airport reading. If that source is stale or unavailable, the page switches to an explicitly labeled planning estimate instead of presenting the estimate as live.

The 30-day hourly pattern adds context that a single number cannot: a line that looks fine right now may overlap a normally busy departure bank, which changes the buffer you should keep.

What "Official Airport Systems" Means

Airport authorities and management companies may publish traveler-facing wait data produced by checkpoint sensors, staffing systems, queue monitoring, or their own estimates. TSA Tracker reads verified public airport-controlled sources where an integration is supported.

That is materially different from crowdsourced reports or predictive estimates. Those can still be useful in some settings, but they are not the same thing as a live checkpoint reading.

Not every airport publishes this data publicly. Supported pages therefore show a fresh live state only when a recent source row is available and otherwise keep the fallback label visible.

Why Official Feeds Come First

Airport security lines change quickly, and user-submitted reports can be late, sparse, or biased toward unusual experiences. Official airport feeds are not perfect, but they are the most defensible starting point because they come from the systems airports already use to monitor their own checkpoints.

That sourcing choice also makes the site easier to audit. Each airport page links back to official airport resources where possible, and the methodology page explains how TSA Tracker handles stale feeds, fallback estimates, and airport-specific caveats.

Lane Types Explained

Where airports provide lane-level data, TSA Tracker shows separate wait times for each lane type:

  • Standard — the regular TSA security line open to all passengers.
  • TSA PreCheck — expedited screening for enrolled travelers. Shoes stay on, laptops stay in bags. PreCheck lines are almost always shorter than standard, often dramatically so during peak hours.
  • CLEAR — a paid biometric identity verification service that can skip the ID-check step. CLEAR members still go through TSA screening, but the identity portion moves faster.

Not all airports report separate wait times by lane. Some only publish a single aggregate figure for the whole checkpoint. In those cases, we display the single available number.

Color Coding Guide

Each checkpoint card uses a simple color system to help you immediately understand conditions:

  • Green — short wait, generally under 15 minutes. You're in good shape.
  • Yellow — moderate wait, roughly 15–30 minutes. Factor this into your timing.
  • Red — long wait, over 30 minutes. Arrive earlier than you planned, or check a different checkpoint if your airport has multiple security entrances.

Tips for Getting Through Security Faster

  • Check the history chart before you leave home. If you're flying at 8 AM and the chart shows consistent red between 6–9 AM, leave earlier or prepare for a long line.
  • Consider TSA PreCheck. Compare the official providers, current pricing, and the standard-versus-PreCheck waits at airports you actually use before deciding whether enrollment is worthwhile.
  • Know your airport's checkpoints. Large airports like ORD, LAX, and JFK have multiple security checkpoints in different terminals. One might have a long line while another is clear. Check your terminal before committing to a checkpoint.
  • Pack smart. The biggest cause of slow screening (beyond volume) is bags that need secondary inspection. Keep liquids accessible, laptops easy to pull out, and avoid wearing heavy metal accessories.
  • TSA recommends 2 hours for domestic, 3 for international. That's a minimum — if wait times are high during your window, add more buffer.

Accuracy and Limitations

TSA Tracker preserves the value and timestamp supplied by each supported source, but that does not guarantee the wait a particular traveler will experience. Important caveats include:

  • Some airports update their feeds less frequently than others. Where we can detect stale data, we flag it.
  • Wait times can change rapidly during peak periods. A 5-minute wait can become 25 minutes in the time it takes you to park and get to the terminal.
  • TSA Tracker shows data per checkpoint, not a prediction of what wait times will be when you arrive. Use the history chart for forward-looking planning.
  • A small number of airports use estimated or sensor-averaged data rather than real-time queue measurement. We note this where we're aware of it.

Editorial Standards

TSA Tracker does not accept payment to alter wait-time readings, airport rankings, or source labels. Sponsored links and advertising, where present, are separate from the live-data layer and do not affect what the airport cards or history chart show.

We prefer narrower but more defensible coverage over broad pages with weak sourcing. If an airport does not have a reliable public source, we would rather leave it out or mark it as fallback than publish a number we cannot support.
